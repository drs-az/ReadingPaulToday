Why a Guide to Paul? Big picture

Paul’s letters shape almost every area of Christian life: the gospel, the church, sexuality, money, spiritual gifts, gender, and more. He wrote as a missionary Jew to communities of both Jewish and Gentile believers, often struggling with cultural differences and false teachings.

For many Christians, his writings are both deeply life-giving and deeply confusing. Some verses are engraved on coffee mugs; others are flashpoints in church debates.

This guide assumes two things at the same time:

  • Paul’s writings are inspired Scripture and carry enduring authority for the church.
  • Paul’s letters are occasional, contextual documents — written to real churches, in specific cultures, to address concrete problems.

Faithfulness means holding both: we don’t dismiss Paul as “outdated,” and we don’t rip sentences out of context and apply them in ways that contradict the gospel he preached.

Key idea: Paul doesn’t compete with Jesus. Paul is a Spirit-inspired witness who helps us see what it looks like for Jesus to be Lord in real communities, with real sin, pain, and hope.

Framework: How to Read Paul Today Orientation grid

Before we zoom into specific topics, here is the “map” you carry with you whenever you open one of Paul’s letters.

1. Christ at the center

Paul consistently points away from himself to Christ. Jesus’ death and resurrection, not Paul’s personality or preferences, are the core. Any reading of Paul that:

  • Contradicts Jesus’ character or teaching, or
  • Makes Paul the center instead of Christ,

is already off-course.

2. Letters, not abstract textbooks

Paul writes to churches with specific problems: division in Corinth, legalism in Galatia, persecution in Thessalonica, false teaching in Colossae, and so on. So we ask:

  • What was the situation he was speaking into?
  • What is the timeless principle underneath his specific instructions?
  • How might the same principle look in our context?

3. Already / not yet

Paul sees Christians living between two worlds: the old age (sin, death, broken systems) and the new age launched by Christ’s resurrection. Some of his instructions lean toward stability in a broken world; others lean toward signs of the coming kingdom.

4. Whole-Bible coherence

Paul reads the Old Testament constantly. We read Paul best when we:

  • Notice when he is echoing the Torah, Psalms, or Prophets.
  • Let Scripture interpret Scripture, rather than isolating him from the rest of the Bible.
Practice: Before you debate a single verse from Paul, read the whole chapter (or letter) and ask: “If I had only this letter, what would I think Paul is most passionate about?”

Salvation & Justification by Faith Saved by grace

What Paul teaches

“Everyone has sinned and falls short of God’s glory, and all are justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus” (Rom. 3:23–24). Paul insists we are saved by faith in Christ, not by human effort. He says plainly that “a person is not justified by works of the Law but through faith in Jesus Christ.”

This was radical in his day because he was addressing debates about whether Gentile converts must adopt Jewish law (circumcision, kosher food, Sabbath) to be truly in God’s covenant family. Historically, Romans was written to a divided church of Jews and Gentiles in Rome; many Jewish Christians expected all to obey Torah laws, causing tension as some Gentile believers felt enslaved by old customs.

Paul’s reply was that God’s promise has always come by grace through faith. He pointed to Abraham, “who believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness,” meaning even the patriarch was accepted by faith, not law.

Theologically, Paul means that God’s righteousness (His just and faithful character) is revealed in Jesus. Through the cross and resurrection Christ dealt with our sin so that trusting in him makes us right with God. Jesus became what we are so that we might share in what he is. In other words, Jesus’ sacrifice is the only ground of our justification; human merit plays no part.

Binding or contextual?

Paul treats justification by faith as a timeless gospel principle. It is the heart of the gospel. All Christians everywhere are called to trust Christ alone for salvation. However, Paul also insisted that true faith shows itself in love and obedience (for example, Galatians 5:6). Genuine believers will produce fruit even while these works never earn salvation.

How we live this today

We apply this by resting in Jesus’ finished work rather than striving to earn God’s approval. We avoid both pride and despair (since nobody can measure up) and instead live gratefully. Paul told the Galatians to “stand firm…and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery” (Gal. 5:1) – slavery to law-keeping. Likewise, modern Christians find freedom in the gospel by embracing grace, yet out of gratitude we cultivate the fruit of the Spirit (Gal. 5:22–25).

In practical terms, this means we trust Jesus to save us, rejoice in that gift, and obey God motivated by love, not legalism. It also calls us to preach this gospel to all people exactly as Paul did, confident it is “the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes” (Rom. 1:16).

Law, Grace & Christian Freedom No longer under a tutor

What Paul teaches

Paul taught that the Mosaic Law was never God’s ultimate path to righteousness. In Galatians, he protested against teachers who insisted Gentiles be circumcised and follow Torah laws to be saved. He argued instead that Christ fulfills the Law, and now the Spirit enables believers to live righteously by love.

Paul compared the Law’s purpose to that of a tutor or guardian leading to Christ (Gal. 3:24). Once people come to faith in Christ, they inherit God’s promises by faith, not by the old boundary markers. In Romans, he acknowledges the value of the Law to highlight sin, but says it cannot justify anyone.

He wanted Christians to understand that God’s grace through Christ supersedes the old covenant. Believers are no longer slaves but sons and daughters through faith. Thus he exhorted us to live by the Spirit rather than by the flesh, which produces the fruit of the Spirit (Gal. 5:16–25). Rather than obeying rules to earn God’s favor, Christians obey out of inner transformation by the Spirit.

Binding or contextual?

The principle of grace over ritual law is universal. The old ceremonial laws (diet, festivals, circumcision) were specific to first-century Judaism and pointed ahead to Christ, so they are no longer binding in detail. Paul calls observance of those as a requirement for salvation a “yoke” from which Christ freed his followers.

However, the underlying moral truths (honesty, justice, mercy, fidelity, compassion) reflect God’s character and remain binding. Paul repeatedly said the Spirit produces behavior even surpassing what the old Law demanded. He lists love as the fulfillment of the Law (Rom. 13:8–10, Gal. 5:14).

How we live this today

Paul’s teaching frees us from legalism while urging love as our guide. In matters of food, music, worship style, and other disputable issues, each believer can act according to conscience, so long as we do not cause other Christians to stumble (1 Cor. 8–10; Rom. 14). The timeless principle is love-driven obedience, not bondage to form.

In a work setting, for example, one might choose to observe or not observe a special fast, but should do so respectfully. In a mixed congregation, one should accommodate weaker consciences. Overall, the gospel of grace empowers us to obey God willingly, without constantly asking, “How much of the old Law do I need to keep now?” Instead we live grateful, Spirit-filled lives of service.

Christian Morality & Love Ethics from the gospel

What Paul teaches

Paul’s letters teem with moral instruction, but always rooted in the gospel. He repeatedly says love is the highest law. For instance, he writes that “love is the fulfilling of the law” (Rom. 13:10) and that Christians are to “walk in love, as Christ loved us” (Eph. 5:2).

In Galatians he lists the fruit of the Spirit – love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control – as the evidence of true Christian life. In 1 Corinthians 13 he teaches that even the greatest spiritual gifts are worthless without love. He defines love as patient and kind, not envious or boastful, bearing all things and enduring all things.

Paul’s context was often church life gone awry. In Corinth, believers were quarreling, living immorally, and even treating communion irreverently. He rebukes sexual immorality by saying “flee from sexual immorality” (1 Cor. 6:18) and reminds them that their bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit. He warns that sins like greed, impurity and idolatry will exclude people from God’s kingdom (1 Cor. 6:9–10).

He also insists that a little unchecked sin corrupts the community, so he calls the church to confront serious sin (1 Cor. 5), always with restoration as the aim.

Binding or contextual?

Paul’s ethical principles – love, holiness, honesty, sexual purity, forgiveness, mercy – are timeless. His insistence that marriage is honored, that Christians abstain from adultery and exploitation, and that we speak truthfully all reflect patterns God built into creation.

Some concrete instructions had a cultural angle. For instance, Paul’s advice on head coverings (1 Cor. 11) and his restrictions on disruptive speech in some gatherings (1 Tim. 2:11–12; 1 Cor. 14:34–35) were given in specific settings. We must discern which commands express universal truth (for example, modesty, mutual respect, order) and which are culturally conditioned forms.

How we live this today

Practically, Paul’s moral teachings call us to live out the love of Christ in community. That means pursuing justice, personal holiness, and reconciliation. His call to love and honor one another should guide business ethics, family life, sexuality, and conflict resolution.

In church gatherings, following Paul, we ensure order and edification: everyone may speak or pray, but it must be for strengthening the church. His long exposition of love encourages us that our motivation in everything – giving, preaching, serving – must be love.

On a personal level, his teaching pushes us to examine our relationships: Are we patient with others? Do we forgive? Do we use our freedom to serve? Paul would say the most important fruit of any Christian life is love.

The Church: Unity, Gifts & Discipline Many members, one body

What Paul teaches

Paul often compares the church to a single body with many parts (Rom. 12:4–5; 1 Cor. 12:12–27; Eph. 4:4–6). Every believer is a unique “member” of this body and all are equal before Christ. In Corinth, Christians were dividing into camps (Paul vs. Apollos vs. Cephas), and he sharply asks, “Is Christ divided?”

He insists leaders are merely servants and that Christ is the only foundation. He writes that “to each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good” (1 Cor. 12:7) and lists many gifts: wisdom, faith, healing, teaching, tongues, and more. The goal is building up the church, not self-promotion.

Paul emphasizes orderly worship: “Let all things be done for building up” (1 Cor. 14:26). He warns that exercising a gift without love is worse than nothing (1 Cor. 13).

Historical context

Early churches were small communities with big disagreements. In Corinth, some abused spiritual gifts to impress others, causing disorderly worship. In Ephesus and elsewhere, believers had to navigate ethnic and social divisions.

Paul’s message everywhere is that ethnic status (Jew or Greek), social status (slave or free), and gender are secondary to being united in Christ. He famously writes, “In Christ… there is no Greek or Jew, circumcised or uncircumcised, slave or free” – for Christ is all, and in all (Col. 3:11).

Binding or contextual?

The principles of unity and mutual service are timeless: all believers everywhere remain one body under Christ. We must cherish unity and diversity in the church.

Some concrete examples reflect his context. The specific form of church discipline in 1 Corinthians 5 (expelling a moral offender) was suited to a tight-knit city church; today, different contexts might apply the same principle of holiness and care in different ways. Likewise, Paul’s exact lists of spiritual gifts reflect first-century needs. His underlying rule is clear, though: all gifts, whatever their form, are meant to serve the community in love, not to exalt individuals.

How we live this today

Churches today can apply these teachings by promoting servanthood and humility. Paul’s own example – calling himself “the worst of sinners” yet chosen by grace – underlines that leaders should not lord authority but serve.

When choosing church leaders and assigning roles, communities still use Paul’s criteria (see the leadership section) to ensure integrity. We also heed his model of discipline: confronting sin gently but firmly, aiming at repentance and restoration, remembering that those involved are brothers and sisters in Christ.

For individual Christians, remembering we are part of a body means we should both use our gifts and appreciate others’. If you teach, encourage; if you help, rejoice that the body is built up. Paul would ask each of us: “How are you serving your brothers and sisters in Christ?”

Leadership & Church Structure Servant oversight

What Paul teaches

Paul’s letters to Timothy and Titus (the “Pastoral Epistles”) give concrete instructions for organizing churches. He assumes each local church should appoint elders (overseers) and deacons to shepherd the flock. In Titus he charges Titus to “appoint elders in every town,” choosing mature people of blameless character: faithful in marriage, self-controlled, hospitable, able to teach, not greedy or violent.

These leaders were meant to model Christian living and guard sound doctrine. In Crete and Ephesus, false teachers were upsetting households with distorted gospels, and Paul sends Titus and Timothy to restore order.

Paul emphasizes that doctrine shapes conduct. What a congregation believes about Jesus fundamentally determines how it behaves. So the qualifications he lists underline integrity, faithfulness in family life, and commitment to the gospel. These rules were expressed in a patriarchal context (where households were run by male heads), but the underlying moral vision is clear: godly leaders are marked by faith, love, gentleness, self-discipline, and a peaceful home.

Binding or contextual?

The principle of qualified servant leadership is universal. The idea that leaders must be above reproach, hospitable, able to teach, and not domineering is not bound to any one culture.

The exact descriptors (male, married, householders) come from that world. Today, churches still recognize the need for mature leaders who teach sound doctrine and who live exemplary lives. Whether women can serve in these roles is debated: Paul’s instruction that “I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man” (1 Tim. 2:12) is interpreted differently by faithful Christians.

Some see it as limited to a particular situation in Ephesus (where some women were spreading false teaching). Others read it as a universal pattern. Either way, Paul also affirms that men and women are equal in Christ (Gal. 3:28), so the heart of his teaching is that all leadership must be exercised humbly and faithfully, not as domination.

How we live this today

Churches honor Paul’s teaching by carefully training and selecting leaders (pastors, elders, deacons) who meet godly qualifications. Congregations often use 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1 as checklists.

We also follow his emphasis that leaders should not exploit the flock. Paul at times refused financial support so no one could accuse him of greed. All church members, in turn, should submit to loving leadership while leaders must serve as examples, not tyrants.

Paul’s pastoral heart means we restore order gently when problems occur, always with repentance and healing as the goal.

Family, Gender & Sexuality Household under Christ

What Paul teaches

Paul addresses family and sexual ethics in several letters, always urging believers to honor God in these relationships. In household codes (Eph. 5:22–6:9; Col. 3:18–4:1; Titus 2:1–10), he applies gospel principles to marriage, parenthood, and even slave–master relations.

He tells wives to respect their husbands and husbands to love their wives “as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her.” He instructs children to obey parents, and fathers not to provoke their children but to raise them in the Lord. These instructions echo Greco-Roman family expectations but are transformed by Christ’s lordship: authority is reframed as sacrificial service, and submission is reframed as voluntary trust under God.

Paul also deploys powerful family imagery for the church: adoption, inheritance, brothers and sisters, co-heirs of Christ. In Colossians and Galatians he says that in Christ social divisions (Jew/Greek, slave/free, male/female) are relativized; all are one in Christ.

In Philemon he applies this dramatically. Onesimus, a runaway slave, has become a Christian. Paul pleads with Philemon to receive him not as property but “as a beloved brother,” even offering to cover any debt Onesimus owes. The cross levels all distinctions.

On sexual ethics, Paul is explicit. He commands Christians to flee sexual immorality (1 Cor. 6:18) and to honor marriage. In a world of temple prostitution and casual sex, he insists sex belongs within the covenant of marriage (1 Cor. 7). He teaches that the body is for the Lord and the Lord for the body, and that our bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit.

Binding or contextual?

Paul’s call to purity and fidelity is broadly seen as universal. Marriage vows and chastity are treated as God-given values throughout Scripture. The principles behind Paul’s words – that sex is to be treasured within marriage and that everyone should be treated with dignity – remain applicable.

Some specifics may be culturally bound. Paul’s instructions about women in certain church gatherings were given in particular contexts where some women were being misled or were disrupting worship. Faithful readers disagree on how far these should be generalized.

Whatever one’s view, Paul’s overarching ethic is mutual submission “out of reverence for Christ” (Eph. 5:21) and service to one another. His core message is not that some gender dominates, but that all believers imitate Christ’s self-giving love in their roles.

How we live this today

Paul’s emphasis on equality in Christ invites us to treat each person (man or woman, slave or free) with full respect. In marriage, his command to husbands and wives is usually read in light of mutual sacrificial love: both are called to lay down their lives as Christ did.

Children and parents still follow Paul’s advice to honor one another as part of a Christian household. In terms of sexuality, the clear application is sexual fidelity within marriage and abstinence outside it, understanding that our bodies honor God.

This may conflict with prevailing cultural norms, but Paul would say that God’s design for sex is not arbitrary. We also learn to treat coworkers, neighbors, and strangers as image-bearers of God. Paul’s move to call slave and master “brothers” continues to inspire Christians to oppose injustice and uphold human dignity.

Spiritual Gifts & Worship Ordered love

What Paul teaches

Paul’s teaching on worship is practical and centered on love. In 1 Corinthians 11–14 he addresses chaotic services in Corinth where some were hogging attention by speaking in tongues or prophesying loudly.

He reminds them that worship is for God’s glory and the building up of others. Though many kinds of Spirit-given gifts exist, they should be exercised for the common good, in order, and motivated by love. He permits speaking in tongues only if an interpreter is present, so the church can understand.

He scolds those who seek ecstatic experiences at the expense of newcomers hearing the gospel and tells them to prefer intelligible words that help others.

Above all, he elevates love as the greatest gift: 1 Corinthians 13 concludes that faith, hope and love abide, but the greatest is love. Even the most dramatic spiritual gift is nothing if it does not serve in love.

Binding or contextual?

The practical principles of Paul’s worship teaching are universal: orderliness, mutual respect, and love must rule our gatherings. How exactly that looks can vary. Paul doesn’t prescribe one fixed liturgy, but he insists the motive is to honor God and care for others.

Issues like head coverings, seating arrangements, or precise liturgical forms are culturally bound. What remains binding is the spirit: use whatever gifts God has given you in a loving and humble way, so that when you come together, all things are done for building up.

How we live this today

Churches apply Paul by encouraging members to discover and use their spiritual gifts in service, not for personal glory. Leaders often use Paul’s lists (Romans 12, 1 Corinthians 12, Ephesians 4) to help people identify gifts.

In worship services, many congregations adopt policies inspired by Paul: anyone can pray or testify, but usually through a shared structure so as not to create confusion. Teaching the Word is central, so that everyone understands.

Above all, communities intentionally foster “one another” relationships as the practical outworking of all gifts. The Holy Spirit can move powerfully, but should never produce confusion or pride; everything must give glory to Jesus and love to neighbors.

The Holy Spirit & Sanctification Life in the Spirit

What Paul teaches

Paul consistently teaches that the Holy Spirit is the source of Christian life. Receiving the Spirit brings believers into God’s family, marks them as belonging to Christ, and empowers them to live differently.

He contrasts living “according to the flesh” (our old sinful nature) with living “by the Spirit” (Rom. 8:5–13). In Galatians, he says that trusting Christ and living dependently on the Spirit produces the fruit of the Spirit: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control.

Paul adds that this life requires daily cooperation: “If we live by the Spirit, let us also keep in step with the Spirit” (Gal. 5:25) – in other words, we must prune sinful habits and cultivate new virtues. The Spirit writes God’s law on our hearts and changes us from the inside out.

Binding or contextual?

Believing in and seeking the Holy Spirit’s work is universal to Paul’s teaching. Salvation is both justification (by faith) and sanctification (by the Spirit).

Specific manifestations of the Spirit (tongues, healings, prophecies) are debated among Christians, but Paul sees all gifts as coming from one Spirit for building up the church. No gift is meant to operate without love or self-control.

How we live this today

We apply this teaching by relying on the Spirit’s power rather than our own resolve. Prayer, Scripture, and fellowship are means by which the Spirit nurtures us.

When facing temptation, we remember Galatians 5:16 – “walk by the Spirit, and you will not gratify the desires of the flesh” – and intentionally lean on God for strength. Small-group communities and discipleship relationships are ways we “carry one another’s burdens” through the Spirit’s love.

Practically, this can look like confessing sin to one another, praying for one another’s healing and growth, and asking God regularly to fill us with his Spirit (Eph. 5:18).

The Cross & Resurrection Center of Paul’s gospel

What Paul teaches

At the heart of Paul’s theology is the cross and resurrection of Jesus. He often summarizes the gospel as “Christ died for our sins… he was raised on the third day” (1 Cor. 15:3–4). Paul says in preaching he decided “to know nothing except Jesus Christ and him crucified” (1 Cor. 2:2).

He explains that Jesus took our penalty upon himself and by rising again conquered sin and death for us. As a result, “in Christ we become new creations” (2 Cor. 5:17). By faith we share in his death and resurrection life (Gal. 2:20).

Paul teaches that if Christ has not been raised, then our faith is futile and we are still in our sins (1 Cor. 15:17). The resurrection means the old age is passing away and a new creation has begun.

Binding or contextual?

The death and resurrection of Jesus are the core of Christian faith for all time. Paul’s letters make clear this is not cultural or optional. “Jesus is Lord” (the risen Lord) is the confession at the center of the Christian story.

Christians can debate some details about how the resurrection will unfold, but the fact of Christ’s resurrection and its saving significance is absolutely central and timeless.

How we live this today

Paul’s teaching on the cross and resurrection undergirds all his other instructions. It assures Christians that justice will ultimately prevail and that God has dealt decisively with sin.

This inspires gratitude, humility and courage. Even when persecuted or facing death, Christians can participate in Jesus’ life with confidence. It also guides mission: Paul would not water down the cross, even when it was foolishness to some and offensive to others. Today, it calls believers to preach Christ crucified and risen above all else.

This transforms priorities: comforts, health, and status are not ultimate. Serving the gospel and becoming like Christ are.

Suffering, Endurance & the Return of Christ Hope under pressure

What Paul teaches

Paul frequently writes about facing trials with hope. He sees suffering as sharing in Christ’s own sufferings (Phil. 3:10; Col. 1:24) and as a means to testify to God’s power (2 Cor. 12:9–10).

In Philippians he even says, “to live is Christ, and to die is gain” (Phil. 1:21) – meaning that being alive lets him serve Jesus and others, while dying would take him to be with Jesus, which he counts as gain.

Regarding the Second Coming, Paul wrote 1–2 Thessalonians to churches anxious about the return of Jesus. He teaches that Jesus will return visibly to judge and restore the world. He reassures suffering believers that their persecution will not last forever and that God will grant relief and justice.

He also corrects false rumors that the “day of the Lord” had already come, reminding them that Christ’s coming will follow certain events, and that they should not be shaken by sensational claims.

Binding or contextual?

Paul’s hope in the resurrection of the dead and Christ’s return is universal for Christians. The general outcome – that Christ will judge evil and vindicate the faithful – is core to Christian faith.

The timeless principle is to live expectantly and ethically, not being idle or despairing. The precise timeline or identity of end-time figures remains mysterious; Paul’s focus is not on speculation but on steadfast faith and love.

How we live this today

For modern Christians, Paul’s eschatology means our hope shapes our living. We remain confident that injustice will be set right and that persecution is not ultimate defeat.

Practical steps include standing firm in trials, praying for one another, and focusing on Christlike living rather than apocalyptic thrill-seeking. Christians might debate precise end-time timelines, but Paul’s advice is clear: work, love, and serve as long as there is breath.

He also tells believers to comfort one another with the promise that the dead in Christ will rise and that we will be with the Lord forever (1 Thes. 4:16–18). This helps believers face death or loss with hope.

Spiritual Example: Joy, Humility & Generosity Paul’s way of life

What Paul teaches

Throughout his letters Paul models and instructs a Christlike attitude in daily life. A standout example is his letter to the Philippians, sent from prison: even in chains Paul exults, “Rejoice in the Lord always; again I will say, rejoice!” (Phil. 4:4).

He teaches that true contentment and joy come from Christ, not circumstances. He writes, “for me to live is Christ, and to die is gain,” meaning that life and death are both defined by belonging to Jesus.

He also gives us the famous Christ hymn in Philippians 2:6–11, which says Christ “emptied himself” by becoming human and obedient to death, even death on a cross. Paul urges believers to have this same mindset – valuing others above themselves and serving sacrificially.

Paul teaches generosity too. In 2 Corinthians 8–9 he exhorts the churches to give to the poor with a willing heart, emphasizing that God loves a cheerful giver and that financial generosity is part of Christian worship. In Philippians he commends the believers for supporting him, calling their gift “a fragrant offering, an acceptable sacrifice, pleasing to God.”

Binding or contextual?

The principle of imitating Christ’s humility and love is universal. Christians everywhere are called to selflessness and joy in suffering, not despair or bitterness.

The specifics (such as Paul’s personal choice to remain single or to decline support in some places) are his own, but the underlying lessons are timeless. His commands to rejoice, give thanks in all things, and consider others as more significant than ourselves apply across cultures.

How we live this today

We follow Paul’s example by nurturing joy in Christ even when life is hard. This might mean focusing on prayer and thanksgiving amidst trials, as Paul did.

We also practice humility: in workplaces or churches, we can “count others more significant than ourselves” by genuinely listening, serving, and not insisting on our preferences.

Paul’s teaching on generosity inspires us to help those in need, support ministry, and share resources. He sees these acts as tangible expressions of the gospel’s love and as sacrifices pleasing to God.

Social Ethics: Slavery, Work & Government Faith in public

What Paul teaches

Although Paul did not initiate social revolutions, his letters contain seeds that undermine injustice. He never commands Christians to revolt against Roman slavery or empire, but he transforms personal relationships in ways that point beyond those systems.

In Philemon, we see him gently subvert slavery. Onesimus, a runaway slave, becomes a Christian under Paul’s ministry. Paul writes to Philemon, Onesimus’s owner, to forgive the slave and receive him “no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, as a beloved brother.” He even offers to pay back any wrong Onesimus has done. This models Christ absorbing the punishment for sinners and implies that the slave is equal to his master before God.

Regarding work and wealth, Paul advises sobriety and diligence. In Thessalonica he commends those who labor for their living and sternly tells the idle to work (2 Thes. 3:10). In Ephesians he calls believers to do honest work with their hands so they can share with those in need. To the rich he warns not to trust in wealth but to be rich in good works, generous and ready to share (1 Tim. 6:17–18).

On government, Paul writes in Romans 13 that every person should be subject to the governing authorities, for there is no authority except from God. He says taxes should be paid and that rulers are meant to be God’s servants for justice. This was bold in an empire where power could be brutal.

Paul’s counsel is that Christians should normally be good citizens, honoring laws that do not force them to sin, while remembering that human authorities are accountable to God.

Binding or contextual?

The principle of mutual respect, responsible citizenship, and concern for justice holds across ages. Paul’s advice to honor leaders and authorities is generally seen as universal, with the caveat that we must obey God rather than humans when the two conflict.

He does not explicitly outlaw slavery, but by declaring that in Christ there is no slave or free, and by his appeal in Philemon, he plants the seeds for Christians to eventually reject slavery as incompatible with the gospel. His ethic is relationally subversive: master and slave share one Lord.

How we live this today

Believers today apply Paul’s social teachings by advocating equality and justice and by treating everyone (employees, neighbors, refugees, the poor) with full human dignity.

In workplaces, we follow Paul’s ethic by doing honest work and using our wealth compassionately. Governments are to be respected insofar as they allow godly living; Christians pray for leaders and seek laws that protect the vulnerable and promote peace.

Ultimately, Paul’s teachings on social order remind us that faithfulness to Christ transcends, but also transforms, social systems. We live with integrity under civil law, yet our primary allegiance is to Christ as King.

Eschatology: The Kingdom & Final Things Living between the times

What Paul teaches

Paul teaches that God’s story will reach its climax in the return of Christ and the renewal of creation. He expects Jesus to return and to inaugurate God’s eternal kingdom.

In Philippians 3:20 he writes, “Our citizenship is in heaven,” indicating that Christians live as exiles on earth awaiting their true King. In Romans 8:18–25 he describes all creation groaning in expectation of the revealing of the children of God – a clear eschatological hope.

Paul’s message is that every believer lives between the times: we have been saved and given the Spirit, yet we await a future glory that will far outweigh our present sufferings.

Binding or contextual?

Belief in Christ’s return and the resurrection of the dead is a core article of Christian faith. Paul taught it to all the churches; denying it was a serious error to him.

Details (the timing, specific end-time figures, or interpretive schemes) have been debated for centuries, but none of those alter the basic call: to live now as citizens of the coming kingdom.

How we live this today

Christians apply this by living with eternity in view. We invest our time and resources in spiritual growth, mission, and justice, not merely in self-indulgence.

We comfort one another with the hope that death is not the end, that God will right all wrongs, and that the glory to come will eclipse present pain.

Paul’s mindset can be summed up as: “Don’t lay up treasure on earth, but in heaven.” In practice, that means seeking first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, trusting that all else will be added as we need it.

Applying Any Passage from Paul Field checklist

Use this as a quick diagnostic whenever you’re trying to decide how a particular teaching from Paul lands today.

1
Locate the passage in the flow of thought.
Read the whole chapter (or letter) around it. What problem is Paul addressing? What argument is he making?
2
Identify the timeless principle.
What underlying truth about God, the gospel, or human relationships is being taught? Separate that from the cultural “clothing” it’s wearing.
3
Check coherence with Jesus and the rest of Scripture.
Does your interpretation fit with Jesus’ teaching and the broader biblical witness? Scripture won’t genuinely contradict itself.
4
Account for genre and occasion.
Is this a general instruction, a situational fix, or a proverb-like wisdom line? Are there local factors (false teachers, persecution, cultural practice) driving the specific wording?
5
Discern form vs. principle.
Is the specific practice (for example, head covering) the enduring command, or is it one way of expressing an enduring principle (for example, honoring God and avoiding scandal)?
6
Test the fruit.
If lived out as you’re applying it, would this bear the fruit of the Spirit and reflect the character of Christ? Or would it tend toward fear, domination, or pride?
Remember: The goal is not to make Paul say what we wish he said, nor to apply him in a way that contradicts the gospel. The goal is to let the Spirit, through Paul, form us into people who look more like Jesus.
